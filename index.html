Proposition: Climate change is real: global temperatures have risen meaningfully since the mid-20th century.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Showing a single year, 2024 (−0.5). We mapped only 2024, the hottest year in the dataset (World +2.12 °C, well above 2023's +1.78 °C). It was also the record year for 124 of 225 countries. Choosing the peak makes the map as red as it can be: the median country is +1.87 °C in 2024 but +1.35 °C averaged over 2015–2024. We considered a 10-year average, which smooths out one-year spikes and is more defensible. The average still shows every country warmer (216 of 216), so 2024 alone adds intensity without changing the conclusion. We used it because "the latest year" sounds like a neutral default.
  • Diverging midpoint shifted to −0.3 °C (−1). A blue–gray–red scale tells the reader that gray means "no change", but our gray sits 0.3 °C below zero. A country with zero change therefore already looks pale orange. The persuasive effect is that no country can look neutral: even Iceland, the least-warmed country at +0.035 °C, reads as clearly warm. Centering the scale at 0 is the honest choice. We rejected it because Iceland would have been close to gray, giving viewers a visual "exception" to point to.
  • Lopsided, clipped color range, −1 to +3 °C (−1). The blue side of the scale stops at −1 and goes unused, since no country was below 0 in 2024, while the red side has five shades. Everything above +3 °C (20 countries, up to +3.69 °C) is drawn in the darkest red, and the legend reads "+3 or more". Spending nearly all the color resolution on warming makes the map look uniformly dark red. A symmetric range such as −4 to +4 °C would be standard for a diverging scale, but most countries would then look medium orange and the map would feel less urgent. Keeping a short blue arm in the legend implies the scale could have shown cooling, which makes the all-red map look like a finding rather than a design choice.
  • A map rather than a ranked chart (−0.5). Maps give each country space in proportion to its land area. The largest countries (Russia, Canada) are at high latitudes, which warm fastest, so they get the darkest colors and the most space. The map therefore looks hotter than the typical country is. A sorted bar or dot chart would weight Iceland and Canada equally and show the real spread from +0.04 to +3.69 °C. We chose the map because it is easy to read at a glance and familiar from climate reporting, so the extra weight on the Arctic isn't obvious.
  • Text and labelling (+1). The headline "Nearly Every Nation Is Running Hotter Than Its Past" is emotive but literally true. If anything it undersells the data, since "nearly" covers countries with no data (e.g., Türkiye, Yemen). The subtitle states the year, baseline and unit, the "213 of 213 countries with data" callout is exact, the source is cited, and missing countries are hatched gray and labelled "No data" rather than hidden. We considered a stronger claim such as "Every Nation…", but the accurate, careful-sounding text is what makes the color choices above believable.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Plotting year-over-year change instead of temperature (−2). Each bar is one year's world temperature minus the previous year's. This transformation removes the long-term level entirely. The world went from +0.21 °C (1961) to +2.12 °C (2024), but warming steps (average +0.25 °C) and cooling steps (average −0.23 °C) look nearly the same size, so the chart reads as random up-and-down noise. The bars add up to +1.9 °C, but no reader adds 63 bars in their head. Plotting the anomaly as a simple line would show the rise immediately, which is why we didn't. This is the most deceptive choice in the project.
  • Leaving out any trend or running total (−1.5). We deliberately drew no cumulative line or fitted trend. Either would turn the "noise" into an obvious upward climb, about +0.03 °C per year on average, which adds up over six decades. Charts of noisy data often include a trend line, so we considered adding a short-window moving average that would look like due diligence while staying flat. We dropped it because even that drifts upward. Omitting the trend is hard to spot because the chart has nothing missing on its face.
  • Headline and callout (−1). "Global Temperatures Fall Almost as Often as They Rise" and "28 of 63 years were cooler than the year before" are both literally true: 28 cooling years, 34 warming years, and 1965 unchanged. The framing invites readers to treat 28 vs. 34 as a coin flip and stop there. A count of directions says nothing about size or accumulation: the cooling-year rate is the same before and after 1993 (14 of 32 vs. 14 of 31) even as overall warming accelerated. We colored the callout blue to match the cooling bars. A headline like "Temperatures Are Stable" would be false and easy to catch, so we picked a frequency claim we could defend.
  • Y-axis symmetric around zero (−0.5). We gave the axis equal room above and below zero (±0.8 °C), so warming and cooling swings look visually balanced. The actual extremes (+0.60 and −0.50 °C) are close to symmetric, so the distortion is mild, which also makes it hard to challenge. We considered fitting the axis tightly to the data (−0.5 to +0.6), but that would show slightly more space for warming, and the even axis looks more like a neutral default.
  • Credible framing (+1.5). The axis says "Change from previous year (°C)", the subtitle says "year-over-year change", the full 1962–2024 range is shown with no years cherry-picked, and the IMF/FAO/NASA source is cited. Red and blue follow the usual warm/cool convention used in climate charts. Nothing is mislabelled, and a careful reader could figure out what's being hidden. We also tried a single neutral color for all bars, but the red/blue version is more persuasive because the blue bars stand out and are easy to count. That's why we kept it.

Final Reflection

Since our group was going into the design process with the general understanding that global temperature is most likely actually increasing, we were worried that our bias may make it difficult to create visualizations for both sides of the proposition. We anticipated some challenge with incorporating deceptive design decisions when presenting what is generally the truth, and vice versa. In the end, we were able to incorporate both deceptive and earnest features into our visualizations with complete opposite conclusions from the same data. Since deceptive design choices can be used even when visualizing something that is generally true, we had to rethink what ethical data visualization is beyond just presenting true, non-cherry picked information.

Our group believes that “ethical analysis and visualization” should be defined as making visualizations that accurately reflect the overall trend and degree of the corresponding phenomenon in real life. The purpose of ethical data visualization is to present data in a truthful and understandable way for a wider audience, therefore if the general direction and magnitude of the phenomenon communicated by the visualization is true to life, we would say that it is ethical.

From our definition of ethical visualization, we concluded the best measurement for determining the difference between acceptable and misleading design choices was how far the viewer’s understanding deviated from the actual truth. For example, although we used a deceptive color range / midpoint and picked a particularly hot year for comparison in the visualization for the proposition, the overall message that most places are hotter than they were 50-70 years ago is true, so some design choices that serve to exaggerate the feel of this difference can still be considered ethical. However, in our visualization against the proposition, the focus on the same number of years having rising and falling temperatures would reasonably lead the viewer to extrapolate that overall temperature is not increasing, which is untrue, therefore it would be considered unethical.
